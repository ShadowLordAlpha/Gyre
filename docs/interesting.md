Prompt that started this note: a fruit-fly brain was “uploaded” and compared to LM layers; it still behaves like an insect without LM-style training. How does that compare to the mass of connections an LM has to train?

Good clarifying context first: what you're describing is real and recent — this isn't hypothetical. The relevant lineage is the FlyWire connectome (2024, ~125,000 neurons, ~50 million synapses, adult female central brain), built from the FlyWire connectome and machine learning predictions of neurotransmitter identity, with a computational model that predicted motor behavior with 95% accuracy, followed by embodied versions from groups like Eon Systems that closed the loop between static connectome maps and dynamic, embodied intelligence, and then a much bigger release just last week: Google Research and HHMI Janelia released a map on September 3, 2026, detailing over 166,000 neurons and 125 million connections across the brain, optic lobes, and ventral nerve cord. That's almost certainly the "upload" you're referring to.

Here's the actual structural comparison, and where the analogy to language models breaks down.

What a language model's "layers and connections" actually are

A transformer LM's architecture is a stack of near-identical blocks (attention + MLP, repeated N times — GPT-scale models run 32 to 120+ of these), connected by weight matrices that start as random noise. There is no information in the architecture about English, physics, or anything else. All of the functional content — grammar, facts, reasoning patterns — is injected entirely via gradient descent over trillions of tokens, adjusting billions to (in frontier models) over a trillion parameters until the loss function is minimized. The architecture is generic and domain-agnostic; the training run is what makes it a language model instead of a random number generator. Strip the weights and you have nothing.

What the fly connectome actually is

The fly upload is not an architecture that then gets trained — it's the equivalent of pulling the already-trained weights directly out of a working system and running them. The "layers" don't exist in the transformer sense. There's no clean sequential depth; it's a sparse, highly heterogeneous, recurrent graph organized around ~5,000 distinct, genetically-specified cell types wired into functional neuropils (optic lobes, mushroom body, central complex, etc.) with feedforward, feedback, and lateral loops running simultaneously. The "connections" are not learned weight matrices — they're actual measured synapse counts and neurotransmitter identities extracted from electron-microscopy imaging of a real physical brain. That's structural ground truth, not a fitted parameter.

Why no training is needed — and this is the actual answer to your question

The fly's "training" already happened, just not during the simulation and not via backprop. It happened over millions of years of selection acting on the genetic program that builds fly neural circuitry from a fertilized egg. The behavior is baked into the wiring topology itself, because biological wiring serves as a structured initialization instead of random weights, starting from millions of years of evolution rather than noise, and the behavior emerges from the wiring of its brain — nobody trained it, there was no reward signal. When researchers copy that graph into silicon and give it inputs, the same thing happens: the behavior emerges without training. This is structurally identical to taking a fully-trained LM's weight checkpoint and running inference — you wouldn't say the checkpoint "learns" anything at inference time either. The fly brain is a checkpoint. Evolution is the optimizer, deep time is the training run, and reproductive fitness across countless generations is the loss function.

The honest caveat, which the more skeptical coverage gets right

Independent reporting on the Eon Systems demo pushed back hard on the "no training, it just woke up" framing: the simulated brain is not operating as a fly's brain does — it's machine learning clamped into the shape of a fly connectome, with a resting state of 0 Hz being zapped with simple inputs, not virtual sensory data. That's an important corrective. The connectome gives you topology and synapse sign/count, but not conductances, firing thresholds, or timing constants — those get filled in with simplified leaky-integrate-and-fire assumptions or, in some versions (the arXiv graph-model paper), the whole-brain connectome is instantiated as a graph-structured neural controller trained via deep reinforcement learning on top of the fixed topology to get locomotion working. So some of the "no training" claims in viral coverage overstate the case — there's often a thin layer of RL or ML fitting happening on top of connectome-fixed structure, not a pure zero-training emulation. Confidence: moderate — this varies by which specific project you mean, and outlets are inconsistent about which version they're describing.

Bottom line

An LM is architecture-plus-training: generic scaffold, all function acquired via gradient descent on data. The fly upload is structure-as-function: a specific, evolutionarily-optimized wiring diagram that already encodes the fly's entire behavioral repertoire before any "inference" happens, because the training already occurred at the species level over evolutionary time and got hard-coded into synapse-level anatomy. The fly's smaller number of "connections" isn't a weaker version of an LM — it's a vastly more information-dense one, purpose-built for a narrow behavioral repertoire (flight, grooming, taste, mating) rather than general-purpose sequence prediction. Scaling this to mammals is the open problem everyone in this space is chasing next; a mouse brain has about 560 times more neurons than a fly, and mapping that connectome at synaptic resolution is the current bottleneck, not the simulation method.

Yes — and this isn't speculative, it's an active line of published research, not a "wouldn't it be neat if" idea. There are two distinct ways people are already doing exactly this, and they lead to different conclusions.

Approach 1: literally transplant connectome topology into an ANN

Researchers have taken the actual wiring diagram of a real nervous system — so far mostly C. elegans, since it was mapped first and is small (302 neurons) — and used it as the literal architecture of an artificial network instead of a stack of uniform layers. The general framing in the field: rather than a Cambrian explosion of neural network architectures being inspired by ideas from brain connectomics, the field had — until recently — mostly ignored network-science, and this new line of work explicitly designs neural network wiring based on connectomic structures, an intersection of network science, neuroscience, and deep learning. Concrete instantiations include a nematode-connectome-derived CNN structure and "Elegans-AI," which uses the C. elegans connectome topology to build both deep and reservoir-style architectures rather than uniform layer stacks. Results are real but modest — proof-of-concept gains on specific tasks, not anything close to transformer-beating performance. The honest read: worm wiring evolved to run a worm, not to do arbitrary sequence modeling, so transplanting it whole gives you a biologically faithful topology with no particular reason to be good at language or vision.

Approach 2: don't transplant the fly's wiring — steal the principle, evolve your own

This is the more interesting and more directly relevant result, and it was applied specifically to GPT-2-scale language models this year. A Stanford/MIT team built what they call a "model connectome": they trained a dense, randomly-initialized GPT-2 on a large corpus, then iteratively pruned it over six generations, killing 20% of the weakest weights each round and collapsing every surviving weight down to just its sign — no magnitude, just excitatory or inhibitory, exactly like real synapses. The result after six generations retained only 25% of the original weights, encoded almost entirely as a sparse, signed wiring diagram. They then took that connectome as the initialization for a fresh model and trained it on a small, "developmental-scale" corpus (100M tokens, roughly a child's language exposure by age 10).

The connectome-initialized model clearly beat a standard dense randomly-initialized model of the same size on the small dataset, and came closest of all the small-data models to matching a full dense model trained on 40x more data — while using only 25% of the weights. Critically, a control model with the same sparsity pattern but randomly shuffled instead of evolved performed far worse than both, which rules out "it's just smaller so it generalizes better" as the explanation — the specific structure carved out by pruning was doing the work, not sparsity alone. When they scaled the pruned model up to 109M active weights (from a 417M dense parent), it matched a dense 124M model trained on the full large dataset, despite never seeing that large dataset itself. That's the fly-brain principle in miniature: compress the lessons of a huge amount of exposure into a structural prior, then let a much smaller "lifetime" of learning finish the job cheaply. It's also, not coincidentally, more brain-like by other measures — its internal representations aligned better with human reading times and fMRI language-network responses than the dense baseline.

What this means for your actual question — should LMs move away from uniform layer stacks?

There's real momentum toward this already, though it's arriving less through direct connectome-copying and more through architectures that independently rediscover the same lessons the fly's wiring encodes:

Sparse, specialized subnetworks instead of dense uniform layers. Mixture-of-experts models already do a crude version of what fly neuropils do — different specialized sub-circuits activate for different inputs instead of every parameter firing on every token. This is directly downstream of the same "sparsely-gated mixture of experts" line of work the Model Connectomes paper cites as intellectual ancestry.
Recurrence over brute-force attention. State-space models like Mamba move away from transformers' all-to-all attention toward something closer to how a real recurrent circuit propagates signal through time — cheaper, and structurally closer to a connectome's actual dynamics.
Sign-and-structure over magnitude. The Model Connectomes result specifically shows that most of the useful information in a trained network's weights lives in which connections exist and their sign, not their precise magnitude — a finding with a direct predecessor in the "lottery ticket hypothesis" and "supermask" literature, and one that maps almost exactly onto how a real synapse (excitatory or inhibitory, present or absent) works.
Where this hits a wall, and I'd flag this bluntly rather than oversell the analogy

The biggest obstacle isn't conceptual, it's hardware. GPUs and TPUs are built to do dense, regular matrix multiplication extremely fast; an irregular sparse graph with heterogeneous connectivity, closer to what a connectome actually looks like, doesn't map efficiently onto that silicon even when it's theoretically more compute-efficient per unit of function. This is a large part of why the field keeps converging on dense transformer blocks despite decades of people pointing out that biological wiring looks nothing like it — the algorithm-hardware fit currently favors "big and dense" over "small and structured," regardless of which one is smarter per FLOP. Neuromorphic chips (Loihi, TrueNorth) exist precisely to fix this mismatch, but they're not remotely close to displacing GPU-trained transformers at frontier scale. Confidence: high that this hardware constraint is the dominant practical bottleneck, not a lack of good ideas on the architecture side.

The second wall: a connectome is a prior for a specific evolved task. A fly's wiring is exquisitely tuned for flight control, odor tracking, and courtship — not for predicting the next token in arbitrary human text. That's exactly why the successful LM result didn't borrow the fly's or the worm's actual wiring diagram; it grew its own connectome from scratch by pruning a model that had already been trained on language. The lesson worth taking from fly brains isn't "copy this specific graph into your transformer" — it's "structure that's been shaped by extensive prior optimization is a far better initialization than random noise, and you can synthesize that structure computationally instead of hand-copying it from biology." That's the piece of the fly-brain story that's already changing how people initialize language models, right now, in 2026.

The math for the more sparse network should actually map just fine, the issue is just that it maps different and likely needs to be preprocessed before unlike a normal dense block in order to get the same performance

The math maps fine in principle — sparse matrix multiplication is well-defined, nobody's claiming otherwise. Where I'd push back is on "just needs preprocessing first," because that undersells what the actual obstacle is. It's not a one-time reformatting step you do and then get dense-equivalent speed. It's an active, currently-unsolved constraint that a whole subfield is still grinding on in 2026, and the reason is structural, not administrative.

**Why "preprocess it" doesn't close the gap**

Modern tensor cores (the actual matmul silicon on H100/B200-class GPUs) only get hardware acceleration from sparsity in one narrow form: strict 2:4 or 1:2 structured patterns, meaning exactly 2 nonzeros in every group of 4 consecutive elements. That's it. Anything looser than that — which includes essentially all naturally-arising sparse structure, whether it's a biological connectome or the pruned mask from the "Model Connectomes" paper I described earlier — has to be handled by different code paths (CSR, block-sparse row, or newer matching-and-padding schemes), and those paths have a well-documented failure mode: below roughly 70% sparsity, unstructured sparse kernels on GPUs deliver limited speedup or outright slowdowns compared to just running it dense, because of irregular memory access, poor data locality for scattered nonzeros, and the difficulty of load-balancing ragged work across threads. The Model Connectomes result I cited was 75% sparse (25% density) — squarely in the regime where unstructured sparse kernels historically lose to dense compute on real hardware, not the regime where sparsity pays for itself.

The "preprocessing" you're gesturing at is real and is exactly what a cluster of 2026 papers is trying to solve right now — reorganizing an arbitrary sparse matrix into something tile-aligned enough for the sparse tensor cores to touch it. But look at what that preprocessing costs. One approach explicitly draws on the graph-theoretic maximum matching problem just to reshape an unstructured matrix into the rigid 2:4 pattern the hardware demands. Another line of work exists specifically because a naive tile-wise sparse format is fundamentally misaligned with the tiled execution of modern dense matmul kernels, preventing direct fusion without expensive synchronization overhead — so they had to invent a new storage format from scratch. None of this is "preprocess once, done." It's an entire ongoing research program (three separate 2026 papers I pulled up — one at ICML, one at ICCAD, one on SC-class HPC systems — are all independently attacking this same unsolved gap right now), and the honest state of the art is: they're closing the gap, not that it's closed.

**The trade-off you can't preprocess your way out of**

Here's the part that actually matters for your original point: the field already knows the fix that reliably works, and it isn't "clever reformatting of naturally-occurring sparsity." It's constraining the sparsity pattern to be hardware-friendly from the start — structured pruning, fixed block sizes, MoE-style expert routing where each expert is itself a fully dense matrix and only the routing is sparse. That works great, precisely because it never tries to accelerate an irregular pattern after the fact. But that same literature states the cost plainly: structured pruning methods that remove entire blocks or sub-modules are straightforward to accelerate on GPUs, but suffer from sharp accuracy degradation, which is exactly why the field has favored unstructured pruning for accuracy despite unstructured sparsity's wall-clock speedup remaining a persistent unsolved challenge. That's not a preprocessing inconvenience, that's a genuine two-horns dilemma: hardware-friendly sparsity patterns lose you accuracy; accuracy-preserving sparsity patterns (the kind that would actually resemble an evolved connectome, where the specific wiring matters) don't map efficiently to current silicon.

So translate this back to the fly-brain comparison: a real connectome's sparsity pattern is shaped by wiring-cost minimization and developmental rules that have nothing to do with GPU tile boundaries. Forcing it into 2:4 or block-aligned form to get hardware speed would mean imposing a constraint the biology never had to satisfy, which is a real degradation risk, not a translation step. My claim stands at moderate-to-high confidence: the bottleneck isn't that sparse math is hard, it's that hardware only rewards a specific narrow shape of sparsity, and biologically-plausible sparsity generally isn't born in that shape — getting it there is exactly the open problem, not a solved preliminary step.
